OTC vs. Prescription
Hearing Aids

A plain-English comparison of the FDA's over-the-counter category and traditional prescription devices — written for adults who want to choose without guessing.

Back to blog
Hearing Aids May 25, 2026 8 min read Buyer's guide

In August 2022, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration created a new category of hearing aid called over-the-counter (OTC), available without a prescription, fitting, or audiology visit. The change opened up choice and lowered prices for many adults — and it also created a lot of confusion. This guide explains who OTC devices are designed for, what they can and cannot do, and when a prescription hearing aid is still the better choice.

Key takeaways

  • OTC hearing aids are FDA-regulated, intended only for adults 18+ with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss.
  • Prescription hearing aids are programmed to your individual hearing test results and adjusted over time by a licensed professional.
  • The right choice depends on the severity of your hearing loss, your comfort with self-fitting technology, and how much support you want during the first 6–12 months.
  • A free hearing assessment is the only reliable way to know whether OTC is an option for you — or whether you need more.

What the FDA actually changed in 2022

The FDA's 2022 final rule created a regulatory category for OTC hearing aids — sold directly to consumers, with no medical exam, no audiogram, and no professional fitting required. The rule was the result of years of advocacy and a 2017 federal law, and it was designed to make hearing care more accessible and more affordable for the millions of adults who never seek help.

The FDA was specific about three things:

  • OTC hearing aids are only for adults 18 years or older.
  • They are intended for perceived mild to moderate hearing loss.
  • They are not appropriate for severe or profound hearing loss, sudden hearing changes, or children.

This matters because the "perceived" part puts the diagnosis on the buyer. Adults estimate their own hearing loss without a hearing test — and research from the American Academy of Audiology suggests that self-assessment is often inaccurate, in both directions.

How OTC and prescription hearing aids actually differ

Diagnosis and fitting

OTC devices are sold with a standard, self-fit program. Many use a smartphone app to perform a short in-ear hearing check and apply a generic prescription. Prescription hearing aids are programmed using a full audiogram — the standard hearing test that measures your response to specific tones across the speech frequency range — and tuned to your ear shape, hearing thresholds, and listening goals.

Power range

OTC hearing aids are capped in maximum output. The FDA limit ensures they cannot be dangerously loud, but it also means they cannot help adults whose hearing loss exceeds mild to moderate. Prescription devices have a much wider range and can be fit for severe or profound loss.

Customization over time

Hearing changes. Lifestyle changes. The first few weeks with any hearing aid involve adjustment, and most patients benefit from at least one follow-up tuning. Prescription devices are designed to be re-programmed by a professional. OTC devices rely on user adjustments through the device or app.

Support, repair, and follow-up care

Prescription devices typically include in-person support, repair coverage, and counseling about communication strategies. OTC support varies widely by brand — some companies offer phone or chat support, others rely on FAQs.

Cost

OTC devices generally cost between a few hundred and around $1,500 per pair. Prescription devices cost more — often $2,000–$6,000 per pair — but the price typically includes the hearing test, professional fitting, follow-ups, repairs, and ongoing care. Consumer Reports has a helpful side-by-side that puts the total cost of ownership in context, not just the sticker price.

Who is a good fit for OTC?

OTC hearing aids may be a reasonable choice if all of the following are true:

  • You are an adult 18+ with no concerning ear symptoms (no sudden loss, no pain, no drainage, no dizziness).
  • You believe your hearing loss is mild to moderate — for example, you struggle most in restaurants and group conversations but generally manage one-on-one.
  • You are comfortable with smartphone apps and self-troubleshooting.
  • You're open to returning the product if it doesn't work for you.

If those conditions don't all apply, prescription is usually the better path.

When prescription is the safer choice

The NIDCD recommends seeing a hearing professional rather than self-treating in any of these situations:

  • Sudden hearing loss in one or both ears (this can be a medical emergency).
  • Hearing loss in only one ear, or hearing that is noticeably different between ears.
  • Ear pain, pressure, or fluid drainage.
  • Dizziness or balance problems.
  • A history of ear infections, ear surgery, or noise-induced hearing damage.
  • Hearing loss that interferes significantly with work or safety.

A professional consultation rules out medical causes — including reversible ones like impacted earwax — and ensures you're not under- or over-treating a real condition.

The "I'll try OTC first" approach

Many adults reasonably want to try OTC before investing in prescription devices. That can be fine — as long as the product has a clear, generous return policy and you give it a fair trial (typically 30–45 days). What we'd add as a hearing professional: a free hearing test before you buy anything tells you whether OTC is even appropriate for your level of loss. It costs nothing and protects you from spending money on a device that physically can't fix your situation.

A practical decision framework

Here's how we'd think about it as a family:

Step 1 — Rule out reversible causes

Earwax, recent congestion, certain medications, and ear infections can all reduce hearing temporarily. A quick exam separates those from age-related hearing loss.

Step 2 — Get a baseline audiogram

It takes 20–30 minutes. The results tell you the type and severity of your hearing loss, which determines whether OTC is even a viable option.

Step 3 — Compare options openly

A trustworthy hearing professional will tell you when OTC is reasonable for your case. If we recommend prescription, we should be able to explain exactly why — usually it's because your audiogram shows the loss is too significant, asymmetrical, or unusual for OTC.

Step 4 — Plan for the first 90 days

Whichever path you choose, plan for adjustments. Hearing aids work best when paired with a few follow-ups and small tweaks. Make sure your purchase includes — or you have access to — that kind of support.

Frequently asked questions

Are OTC hearing aids the same as personal sound amplifiers (PSAPs)?

No. PSAPs are not regulated as hearing aids and the FDA does not allow them to be marketed for hearing loss. OTC hearing aids are a new FDA-regulated medical device category specifically for adults with mild-to-moderate hearing loss.

Can I use insurance or Medicare for OTC devices?

Generally no. Original Medicare doesn't cover hearing aids at all, and most insurance benefits are tied to a hearing test and prescription. Many Medicare Advantage plans include hearing aid benefits — check your plan or have us review it with you.

Will an OTC device damage my hearing?

The FDA's output limits are designed to make OTC devices safe for the intended user. The bigger risk is using an OTC device for the wrong type or severity of hearing loss, or delaying care for a treatable medical condition.

What if I bought OTC and it isn't working for me?

Return it within your trial window if possible, then schedule a consultation. We can review what isn't working — often it's a fit, programming, or severity issue that points to a better solution.

Medical disclaimer. This article provides general information, not medical advice. If you have sudden hearing changes, dizziness, ear pain, or drainage, see a healthcare provider promptly. Always read product labeling and follow FDA guidance for OTC devices.

Hearing Health

7 Signs It May Be Time to Schedule a
Hearing Consultation

Back to blog
Hearing Health May 11, 2026 · Updated May 25, 2026 7 min read Evidence-based

Hearing changes often happen gradually. Many people adjust without realizing how much energy they are spending to follow conversations, enjoy family moments, or feel comfortable in social settings. According to the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders, around 15% of American adults — about 37.5 million people — report some trouble hearing, yet the average adult waits roughly seven years before seeking help. A hearing consultation is a simple first step that closes that gap.

Key takeaways

  • Hearing loss is one of the most common chronic conditions in U.S. adults, affecting nearly 1 in 3 people 65–74.
  • Most age-related hearing loss develops gradually, making it easy to miss until it affects daily life.
  • Common signs include asking for repeats, struggling in noise, high TV volume, and avoiding phone calls.
  • A free consultation is the easiest way to catch hearing changes early — when intervention is most effective.
7 years Average time between first noticing hearing changes and seeking help, per the Hearing Loss Association of America.

1. You ask people to repeat themselves more often

If "Can you say that again?" has become part of your daily routine, your ears may be working harder than they should. This is especially common when voices are soft, fast, or coming from another room. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) lists this as one of the earliest signs of adult-onset hearing loss, often appearing years before it becomes obvious.

It's worth paying attention to context. Do you ask for repeats in quiet rooms too, or mostly in restaurants? Are certain voices — children, soft-spoken adults — harder than others? Those patterns help a hearing professional understand whether the issue is volume, clarity, or both.

2. Restaurants and gatherings feel exhausting

Background noise can make hearing loss more noticeable. You may hear that people are speaking, but the words feel blurred or incomplete. Over time, that effort can make social events feel tiring instead of enjoyable. Hearing researchers call this listening effort: the cognitive cost of decoding degraded speech, which the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health has studied as a contributor to fatigue, withdrawal, and even long-term cognitive risk.

If you've started declining invitations because "it's too loud in there," it isn't an antisocial habit — it's a hearing signal worth investigating.

3. Family notices the TV or phone volume is high

Volume changes are one of the most visible signs for loved ones. If others regularly mention that the TV, radio, or phone speaker is louder than usual, it is worth checking your hearing. ASHA and other professional groups consistently note that family members often recognize hearing changes before the person experiencing them does — partly because the change is gradual, and the brain adapts.

This sign also matters because pushing volume higher can compound the problem. Loud sound exposure is itself a risk factor for further hearing damage, especially with personal headphones or earbuds. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends keeping personal listening below 60% of maximum volume for no more than 60 minutes a day.

4. You hear speech but miss clarity

Hearing loss is not always about volume. Some people hear sound clearly enough but struggle to separate certain words, consonants, or voices. The technical name for this is speech discrimination, and it usually involves the higher frequencies where consonants like "s," "f," "th," and "sh" live. Modern hearing technology can often help improve that clarity in everyday listening environments by amplifying the specific frequencies you've lost without making everything louder.

If you find yourself nodding along during conversations but not actually catching the words, that's a clarity issue, not a volume issue — and it's exactly what a hearing test is designed to measure.

5. Conversations on the phone are harder

Without facial expressions or lip movement, phone conversations can become more difficult. If you avoid phone calls or switch ears often to understand better, a hearing assessment can give you useful answers. Phone calls strip away the visual cues people unconsciously rely on, so phone difficulty is sometimes the first place hearing loss shows up clearly.

Many modern hearing aids support direct phone streaming through Bluetooth, sending the call audio straight into both ears at the right amplification — a feature that's transformed phone communication for many hearing aid users.

6. You feel left out even when you are present

Hearing challenges can quietly affect confidence. People may stop joining conversations, laugh along without catching the details, or withdraw from moments they used to enjoy. Better hearing care is not only about sound; it is about connection. The CDC notes that untreated hearing loss is consistently associated with greater social isolation and depression risk in older adults.

This is one of the most underrecognized signs because it doesn't feel like a "hearing problem." It feels like fatigue, frustration, or social withdrawal. Family members sometimes notice the change before the person does — a parent who used to chat at family dinners now sits quietly, a spouse who declines outings they used to enjoy.

7. You have not had your hearing checked recently

A routine hearing consultation can be helpful even if you are unsure there is a problem. The American Academy of Audiology recommends establishing a baseline in midlife so that future changes are easier to identify and address. At Virtue Hearing Aid Center, Christopher Medina takes time to understand your lifestyle, explain your options, and answer questions without pressure.

Why earlier matters

Waiting often means more than just discomfort. The 2024 Lancet Commission on dementia prevention identified hearing loss as the largest modifiable midlife risk factor for cognitive decline, accounting for roughly 7% of dementia cases globally. The 2023 ACHIEVE trial from Johns Hopkins found that hearing intervention reduced the rate of cognitive decline by about 48% over three years in older adults at higher risk.

None of this means hearing loss directly causes dementia — but it's a strong signal that hearing care is part of long-term brain health, not just an optional comfort.

What happens at a free consultation?

We talk through your hearing concerns, perform a hearing test (audiogram), examine your ear canals for wax or other issues, review your daily listening needs, explain possible next steps, and discuss insurance or financing options when needed. The visit takes about 30 minutes. There's no obligation to buy anything, and you walk out with clear information about your hearing.

If any of these signs feel familiar, you do not have to wait until hearing becomes a major frustration. A consultation can help you understand your hearing today and protect the moments that matter most.

Frequently asked questions

How often should adults get their hearing checked?

Adults with no known hearing concerns benefit from a baseline test in midlife (around age 50) and follow-up testing every 2–3 years. People with known hearing changes, noise exposure, or family history of hearing loss should test more frequently.

What does a hearing test actually feel like?

It's painless. You'll wear headphones, listen to a series of soft beeps at different pitches, and press a button when you hear them. Some tests also include word recognition in quiet and in background noise. Most visits take about 30 minutes from start to finish.

What if my hearing test is normal?

Excellent — you have a baseline on file, and you'll know whether to be concerned about future changes. Many adults are reassured by a normal test even when family members are worried.

Do I need a referral from my doctor?

For a free consultation at Virtue Hearing, no referral is needed. If you're planning to use Medicare or insurance for a diagnostic hearing exam, the rules vary — we can verify your coverage before your visit.

Medical disclaimer. This article is educational and does not replace personalized care. Sudden hearing loss, hearing loss in one ear only, ear pain, or dizziness should be evaluated promptly by a healthcare provider. Some causes of hearing change — including impacted earwax, ear infections, and certain medications — are reversible.

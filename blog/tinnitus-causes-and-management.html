Tinnitus: Why It Happens
And What Actually Helps

An evidence-based guide to ringing, buzzing, or hissing in the ears — written for adults who are tired of being told to "just live with it."

Back to blog
Tinnitus May 25, 2026 8 min read Evidence-based

Tinnitus — the perception of sound when no external sound is present — is one of the most common reasons adults visit a hearing professional. It can sound like ringing, buzzing, hissing, clicking, or roaring. For some people it comes and goes. For others, it's constant. There is no single cure, but there are real, evidence-based strategies that help most people reduce its impact on daily life.

Key takeaways

  • Tinnitus is a symptom, not a disease — most often a sign that the auditory system has been damaged or stressed.
  • The NIDCD estimates that about 10% of U.S. adults experience tinnitus that lasts at least 5 minutes in any given year.
  • The strongest associations are with hearing loss and noise exposure, but medications, stress, and certain medical conditions can also play a role.
  • Sound therapy, hearing aids (when hearing loss is also present), cognitive behavioral therapy, and noise protection are the most consistently supported approaches.

How common is tinnitus?

The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) estimates that roughly 10% of U.S. adults — more than 25 million people — have experienced tinnitus lasting at least five minutes in the past year. The American Tinnitus Association (ATA) places the broader number, including milder and less frequent tinnitus, at closer to 50 million Americans.

~25 million U.S. adults reported tinnitus lasting at least five minutes in the past year, per NIDCD.

About 20% of people with tinnitus describe it as bothersome enough to interfere with daily life — affecting concentration, sleep, mood, or work. That's the group for whom active management makes the biggest difference.

What causes tinnitus?

The honest answer is that researchers still don't fully understand exactly how tinnitus is generated in the brain. What's well-established is that several factors strongly correlate with it.

Hearing loss

The most consistent association. When parts of the inner ear are damaged, the brain often fills in the silence with phantom sound. Many people are surprised to learn they have measurable hearing loss when they come in for tinnitus — they assumed it was only the ringing.

Loud noise exposure

One-time exposures (concerts, fireworks, gunshots) or repeated occupational noise can damage the tiny hair cells in the cochlea. The CDC and NIOSH consider noise-induced hearing loss one of the most common preventable workplace injuries.

Earwax and ear conditions

Impacted wax, middle ear infections, fluid buildup, or eustachian tube dysfunction can all produce or worsen tinnitus. The good news: these are often reversible.

Medications

More than 200 medications can cause or worsen tinnitus, including high-dose aspirin, certain antibiotics, some chemotherapy drugs, and certain diuretics. The Mayo Clinic maintains a useful overview. Never stop a prescription medication without talking to your prescriber.

Other contributors

Head and neck injuries, TMJ (jaw joint) disorders, cardiovascular issues, untreated high blood pressure, and significant stress can all influence tinnitus. For some people, caffeine and certain foods make it temporarily louder.

When tinnitus is a medical concern

Most tinnitus is not dangerous, but some forms warrant prompt medical evaluation. Contact a healthcare provider if you experience:

  • Sudden onset of tinnitus, especially in one ear only.
  • Pulsatile tinnitus — sound that follows your heartbeat.
  • Tinnitus with sudden hearing loss, dizziness, or balance problems.
  • Tinnitus following a head injury.
  • Tinnitus that is significantly interfering with sleep, mood, or concentration.

What actually helps

There is no FDA-approved cure for most chronic tinnitus, but there are well-supported strategies that meaningfully reduce its impact. The American Academy of Otolaryngology's clinical practice guideline outlines what evidence supports.

1. Treat any underlying hearing loss

This is the single most impactful step for adults whose tinnitus comes alongside hearing loss. Hearing aids restore environmental sound, which often makes the tinnitus seem quieter by contrast. Many modern hearing aids also include built-in sound therapy programs designed specifically for tinnitus relief.

2. Sound therapy

Using soft, pleasant background sound — soft music, nature sounds, white or pink noise — can reduce the contrast between tinnitus and silence. This is especially helpful at bedtime, when the brain has fewer sounds to focus on. A simple bedside sound machine helps many people sleep better within the first week of trying it.

3. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

CBT is one of the most rigorously studied approaches for bothersome tinnitus. It does not change the sound itself, but it changes the brain's reaction to the sound — reducing distress, anxiety, and avoidance. The NIDCD notes CBT as a leading evidence-based option, especially when tinnitus is affecting mood or daily function.

4. Tinnitus retraining therapy (TRT)

TRT combines low-level sound therapy with structured counseling, usually over 12–24 months. It works on the idea of habituation: training the brain to recognize tinnitus as a neutral background signal rather than a threat.

5. Protect what you have

Avoid further noise exposure. The CDC recommends earplugs or earmuffs in environments above 85 decibels (lawn mowers, power tools, loud concerts, gun ranges). Custom-fit ear protection is available through any hearing care professional and is one of the best investments for anyone with tinnitus.

6. Reduce known triggers when possible

Some people notice flare-ups with stress, poor sleep, alcohol, or excess caffeine. Keeping a simple log for two weeks can reveal personal patterns worth adjusting.

What does not help (despite the ads)

The FDA has not approved any pill, supplement, ear drop, or "tinnitus device" sold online as a cure for tinnitus. Many advertised products use language carefully crafted to imply effectiveness without ever proving it in a clinical trial. Before paying for any unproven treatment, check with a hearing professional and review what reputable organizations like the ATA currently consider evidence-based.

A practical 30-day plan

If tinnitus is new or newly bothersome, here is a reasonable starting plan:

Week 1 — Evaluate

Schedule a hearing test. Make sure earwax and any reversible causes are ruled out. Bring a list of your current medications and any recent changes.

Week 2 — Add sound at night

Try a soft sound machine or a quiet fan at bedtime. Your goal isn't to mask tinnitus completely — just to give your brain something else to listen to.

Weeks 3–4 — Build daily habits

Protect your ears in loud environments. Limit caffeine after lunch. Get consistent sleep. Notice — without judging — when tinnitus is louder or quieter.

Beyond 30 days

If tinnitus continues to bother you, discuss longer-term options with a hearing professional: hearing aids if you have hearing loss, sound therapy programs, or referral to a CBT-trained therapist.

Frequently asked questions

Will my tinnitus ever go away?

Sometimes. Tinnitus from temporary causes (wax, infection, medication) often resolves when the cause is addressed. Tinnitus tied to hearing loss is usually chronic, but most people who manage it well report that it bothers them much less over time.

Can hearing aids cure tinnitus?

No, but they often help substantially when hearing loss is also present. Many adults report a meaningful reduction in tinnitus awareness within the first few weeks of consistent hearing aid use.

Is tinnitus a sign of something serious?

Usually not, but the symptoms listed in the "When tinnitus is a medical concern" section above are worth a prompt evaluation. Pulsatile tinnitus and sudden one-sided tinnitus deserve same-week attention.

What about supplements like ginkgo biloba?

Current evidence does not support ginkgo or most other supplements as effective tinnitus treatments. The American Academy of Otolaryngology specifically recommends against routine use of ginkgo, melatonin, zinc, or other supplements as primary tinnitus treatment.

Medical disclaimer. This article is educational and does not replace personalized care. New, sudden, one-sided, or pulsatile tinnitus deserves prompt medical evaluation. Do not stop any prescribed medication based on this article — talk to your prescriber.

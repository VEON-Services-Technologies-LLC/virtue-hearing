Family Care

Helping a Loved One Take the
First Step

Back to blog
Family Care May 11, 2026 · Updated May 25, 2026 7 min read For families

Hearing changes can be emotional. A loved one may feel frustrated, embarrassed, or unsure whether they need help. The most supportive conversations are calm, specific, and focused on connection rather than blame. This guide is written for spouses, adult children, and friends who want to help — without making the person they care about feel cornered. It draws on guidance from the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, the Hearing Loss Association of America, and decades of family-counseling research.

Key takeaways

  • Family members usually notice hearing changes before the person experiencing them does.
  • The average adult waits about 7 years between first noticing hearing loss and seeking help, per HLAA — much of that delay is emotional, not practical.
  • Untreated hearing loss is associated with higher rates of social isolation and depression, per CDC and NIDCD data.
  • Calm, specific, situation-based conversations work far better than generalized complaints or pressure.
  • Offering to attend the first appointment together meaningfully increases follow-through.

Why the conversation is so hard

Hearing loss touches identity. For many adults — especially men and people who pride themselves on independence — admitting hearing changes can feel like admitting age, decline, or loss of capability. The Hearing Loss Association of America consistently identifies stigma and self-image as bigger barriers than cost or access, even though those are often the reasons people give out loud.

Understanding this changes how you approach the conversation. The goal isn't to convince someone they're impaired. The goal is to make a small, low-stakes first step feel possible.

1. Start with moments, not criticism

Instead of saying, "You never hear me," try naming a shared moment: "I noticed at dinner last night the conversation felt harder for you to follow, and I want you to feel included." This keeps the focus on the relationship and on care rather than blame.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association's communication tips emphasize this kind of specific, situational framing — naming the listening environment rather than the person.

Helpful framings to try

  • "I noticed it was hard to hear the grandkids during their visit. Would it be worth a hearing check, just to see?"
  • "The TV volume has been creeping up — I'm not bothered, I just want to make sure your ears are okay."
  • "My friend went in for a free hearing test and said it was easier than expected. Would you go with me?"

Phrases to avoid

  • "You're going deaf." (Triggers defensiveness.)
  • "You always miss what I say." (Generalizes; invites argument.)
  • "Just get hearing aids already." (Skips the listening, jumps to a solution.)

2. Acknowledge what they're hearing about themselves

If your loved one says, "It's not that bad," or "Other people just mumble," resist the urge to argue. Both of those responses are real — many adults genuinely don't realize how much their hearing has changed, because the brain adapts. Try: "That might be true, and I just want to know we've checked. A free hearing test takes 30 minutes and there's no pressure to do anything else."

This validates their perspective while keeping the door open to a small action.

3. Offer to attend the consultation together

A second person can help remember details, ask questions, and describe listening situations at home that the patient might forget. Many patients feel more comfortable when someone they trust is part of the first visit — especially when there are decisions about insurance, financing, or device options to discuss.

For older parents, an adult child accompanying them often makes the experience feel collaborative rather than overwhelming. We see this every week at Virtue Hearing — the patients who come in with a family member almost always have more productive consultations.

4. Make the first step low-pressure

A free consultation does not mean someone has to buy hearing aids that day. It is a chance to understand what is happening, learn what options exist, and decide what feels right. The lower the perceived commitment, the more likely your loved one is to agree.

Concrete things that lower pressure:

  • It's free. No financial commitment.
  • It's short. About 30 minutes.
  • It's diagnostic. A hearing test tells them where they actually stand — which may be reassuring.
  • It's optional what comes next. No one has to do anything after the test.

5. Use better communication habits at home

Whether or not your loved one chooses hearing care, you can make conversations easier right now. The ASHA communication tips are simple but make a measurable difference.

  • Face the person. Visual cues — lip movement, facial expression, gestures — help the brain decode speech.
  • Reduce competing noise. Lower TV or music before starting a conversation. Choose quieter restaurants. Move to a quieter room for important conversations.
  • Speak naturally, not loudly. Shouting distorts speech. Slower, clearer, slightly more deliberate speech helps more than volume.
  • Get their attention first. Don't start talking from another room. Use their name to signal that a conversation is starting.
  • Rephrase rather than repeat. If they missed a word, switching to different words often helps more than louder repetition.

6. Watch for emotional signs, not just hearing signs

Untreated hearing loss is associated with higher rates of social isolation, anxiety, and depression in older adults, per the CDC and the NIDCD. The behavior changes that follow hearing loss can be the most important warning signs:

  • Withdrawing from family gatherings or declining invitations.
  • Talking less during group conversations.
  • Looking exhausted after social events.
  • Becoming more easily frustrated or irritable.
  • Stopping activities they used to enjoy (book club, choir, dinner with friends).

These changes aren't always hearing-related, but when they appear alongside the more obvious signs, they're often a clue that the emotional cost of untreated hearing loss is mounting.

7. Be patient with the timeline

Most adults don't go from "I don't have a hearing problem" to "let's schedule a test" in one conversation. Several gentle, low-pressure mentions over months are often what finally lead to action. That's not failure — it's how people process change.

Sometimes the trigger is something specific: a missed phone call from a grandchild, an embarrassing moment at a family dinner, a doctor's recommendation, or another family member's positive experience with hearing aids. Your role isn't to manufacture that moment — it's to make sure that when it comes, the path forward feels easy.

One small ask that often works

"Would you come with me to a free hearing consultation? You don't have to do anything afterward — I just want us both to have a baseline." Framing it as something you're doing together, with no required next step, removes most of what makes the conversation feel heavy.

Frequently asked questions

My parent refuses to even talk about it. What do I do?

Take the pressure off, and shift focus to behaviors that make conversations easier at home (face them, reduce background noise, get attention first). Mention hearing once every few months without making it the topic of every visit. Many adults come around after a triggering moment — your job is to make sure the path is easy when they do.

Is it okay to schedule the appointment for them?

For an adult, no — schedule together. Doing it for them tends to feel controlling and can backfire. Offering to call together, with your loved one present, works better.

What if they say hearing aids are too expensive?

Cost is rarely the real barrier when raised first, but it's a real concern. A free consultation includes a review of insurance benefits, financing options, and 0% payment plans. Knowing the actual numbers — rather than imagining the worst — often removes this objection.

Should the whole family be part of this conversation?

One trusted person is usually better than several. Crowding the conversation can feel like an intervention. Whoever has the closest, calmest relationship with your loved one is usually the best person to bring it up.

Note. This article focuses on age-related hearing loss in adults. Sudden hearing loss, hearing loss following a head injury, hearing loss with severe pain or dizziness, or hearing loss in children should all be evaluated promptly by a healthcare provider.

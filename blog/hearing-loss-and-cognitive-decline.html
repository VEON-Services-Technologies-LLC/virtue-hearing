Hearing Loss and
Cognitive Decline

What the latest research from The Lancet, Johns Hopkins, and the NIH actually says — and what it means for you or someone you love.

Back to blog
Hearing Health May 25, 2026 8 min read Evidence-based

In 2024, an international team of researchers published a landmark report in The Lancet identifying hearing loss as the largest single modifiable risk factor for dementia in midlife. For people in their 40s, 50s, and 60s, that finding turned a hearing checkup into something more than a comfort issue — it made it part of long-term brain health.

This article walks through what the research actually shows, what it doesn't promise, and what practical steps make sense for adults thinking about hearing care today. It is written for patients and families, not researchers, and every key claim is linked to an official source at the end.

Key takeaways

  • The Lancet's 2024 Commission estimates that addressing 14 modifiable risk factors could prevent or delay nearly half of dementia cases worldwide, with hearing loss as the single largest midlife factor.
  • The Johns Hopkins ACHIEVE trial (2023) found hearing intervention slowed cognitive decline by about 48% over three years in older adults already at risk.
  • Hearing loss does not cause dementia directly, but it likely contributes through reduced brain stimulation, social isolation, and increased cognitive load.
  • Most age-related hearing loss is gradual, painless, and easily missed — which is why baseline screening matters before symptoms feel severe.

How common is hearing loss, really?

According to the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD), about 15% of American adults — roughly 37.5 million people — report some trouble hearing. The rate climbs sharply with age: nearly one in three adults between 65 and 74 has hearing loss, and almost half of adults over 75 are affected.

1 in 3 U.S. adults aged 65–74 has hearing loss, per NIDCD. By age 75, the rate approaches 1 in 2.

Despite how common it is, the average adult waits about seven years between first noticing hearing changes and seeking help, according to the Hearing Loss Association of America (HLAA). Those seven years matter, because the brain is also adapting during that time — often in ways that are harder to reverse later.

What The Lancet 2024 Commission found

The 2024 Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, intervention, and care updated its earlier reports and identified 14 modifiable risk factors. Together, those factors account for an estimated 45% of dementia cases that could potentially be prevented or delayed.

Among the midlife risk factors (ages 45–65), hearing loss was the single largest contributor, attributed to roughly 7% of dementia cases globally. The Commission was careful to use the language of association rather than direct causation, but it recommended hearing aids for people with hearing loss as part of a brain-healthy lifestyle.

The other midlife risk factors include high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, and excessive alcohol intake. The point is not that hearing loss alone determines cognitive outcomes, but that it sits inside a cluster of modifiable factors that compound over time.

The ACHIEVE trial: what we learned about hearing aids

Until recently, much of the hearing-and-cognition research was observational, meaning it could show association but not prove that treating hearing loss makes a real difference. That changed with the ACHIEVE trial, led by researchers at Johns Hopkins and published in The Lancet in 2023.

ACHIEVE followed about 1,000 older adults aged 70–84 with untreated hearing loss for three years. Participants were randomly assigned to either a hearing intervention (best-practice hearing aids and counseling) or a health education control group. In the broader study population, the two groups looked similar over three years. But among participants who were already at higher risk for cognitive decline, hearing intervention reduced the rate of cognitive decline by about 48%.

That single number — 48% — has been widely cited, and it deserves context. It does not mean hearing aids prevent dementia. It means that in older adults already on a vulnerable trajectory, treating hearing loss appears to slow how quickly cognition declines. For families thinking about a parent or grandparent, that may be the most actionable evidence available today.

Why might hearing loss affect the brain?

Researchers describe several plausible mechanisms, and they likely work together rather than in isolation. The Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health has summarized the leading theories:

Increased cognitive load

When sound is degraded, the brain works harder to fill in missing information. Over years, that extra effort may pull resources away from memory and other functions. Hearing scientists sometimes describe this as the brain spending its energy on listening instead of understanding.

Reduced stimulation

Sound is a constant signal to the auditory cortex and connected brain regions. When the signal weakens, the brain can show measurable changes in structure and activity. Hearing devices restore meaningful input that helps maintain those networks.

Social withdrawal

People with untreated hearing loss often avoid restaurants, family events, and phone calls. Social engagement is one of the most consistently identified protective factors for cognitive health, and isolation is a known risk factor in its own right.

What this does not mean

It's important to be honest about what the research does not promise. Hearing aids are not a cure for dementia and they have not been shown to prevent it in healthy adults with normal cognition. Some people with hearing loss never develop cognitive decline, and some people without hearing loss do.

What the evidence does support is that hearing loss is a meaningful, treatable factor that contributes to overall brain health — alongside exercise, blood pressure control, sleep, social connection, and mental engagement. Treating it is a reasonable, low-risk choice that comes with immediate quality-of-life benefits even before the long-term cognitive picture is considered.

Practical next steps

1. Get a baseline hearing test in your 50s

Even if your hearing feels fine, the American Academy of Audiology recommends establishing a baseline so that changes over time are easier to detect and address. A free consultation is enough to start.

2. Don't wait for severe symptoms

The seven-year average delay before seeking help is a habit, not a medical recommendation. If family members are commenting on TV volume or you're avoiding restaurants, that's information worth acting on.

3. Treat hearing as part of a brain-healthy lifestyle

The Lancet Commission's framing matters: hearing care sits next to physical activity, social engagement, blood pressure control, and not smoking. No single factor decides cognitive outcomes, but they add up.

4. Bring family into the conversation

Spouses and adult children often notice hearing changes before the person experiencing them does. Including a loved one in the consultation can make the next steps feel collaborative rather than overwhelming.

Frequently asked questions

Do hearing aids prevent dementia?

No. Current evidence shows hearing intervention may slow cognitive decline in older adults at higher risk, per the ACHIEVE trial. It has not been shown to prevent dementia in healthy adults. Even so, hearing care is a low-risk, high-benefit step for brain health overall.

How early should I start thinking about this?

The Lancet Commission specifically highlights midlife (roughly 45–65) as the window where modifiable factors matter most. A baseline hearing test in your 50s is a sensible starting point even without symptoms.

What if I already have mild cognitive impairment?

Talk to your primary care provider and a hearing professional. Some research suggests hearing intervention may be especially valuable for people at elevated cognitive risk, but care decisions should always be coordinated.

Does Medicare cover this?

Original Medicare (Part B) covers diagnostic hearing exams when ordered by a physician, but not routine exams or hearing aids. Many Medicare Advantage (Part C) plans include hearing benefits. See the Medicare coverage page for current details, or ask our team to review your benefits.

Medical disclaimer. This article is for educational purposes and does not replace personalized medical advice. Hearing changes can have many causes, including reversible ones such as wax buildup, ear infections, or medication side effects. If you notice sudden hearing loss in one or both ears, contact a healthcare professional promptly.
